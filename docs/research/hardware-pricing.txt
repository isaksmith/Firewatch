FIREWATCH: MESH AND SENSOR HARDWARE PRICING
Prepared Friday, November 6, 2026. Rough figures from public retail and
distributor listings, not vendor quotes. Prices exclude sales tax.


1. SUMMARY

- One tower node (mesh relay with smoke, temperature, and humidity sensors)
  costs about $157 in parts.
- A county needs six nodes, a station radio, and one optional duty-officer
  radio: about $1,040 in hardware, or about $1,140 with 10% spares. There are
  no monthly fees. The target was under $1,500.
- With the Kansas Forest Service Volunteer Fire Assistance grant (50/50 cost
  share) and a joint purchase across counties, the district's own share is
  about $450-550.
- Six nodes cover roughly 300-600 sq mi with mesh radio, the whole district
  with local temperature and humidity, and about 20-60 sq mi a day with smoke
  detection (the downwind side of the six highest-risk spots).
- Dryad Networks' Silvanet sensor is cheaper per unit (about EUR 48) but is a
  different device: a 100 m gas sniffer that needs EUR 371 gateways for range.
  Covering a 450 sq mi district at Dryad's density would cost about
  EUR 1.2 million plus about EUR 180,000 a year.

4. WHAT THE MONEY COVERS

  Coverage                     Per node                  Six nodes
  ---------------------------  ------------------------  -----------------
  Mesh radio, tower to a       ~5-10 km radius           ~300-600 sq mi
    handheld on the ground       (~30-120 sq mi)           after overlap
  Temperature and humidity     ~15 km radius             The whole district
  Smoke                        ~3-5 km, downwind only    ~20-60 sq mi a day

  Radio: antennas 30-40 m up on elevators and water towers reach each other
  20-30 km apart; a ground-level handheld is the weak link. Creek bottoms and
  rolling Flint Hills ground will leave dead spots. Model the tower sites in
  the Meshtastic Site Planner and drive-test one node before buying six.

  Weather: humidity and temperature change slowly over open grass. Firewatch
  uses a sensor's reading for any live event within 15 km, in place of the
  gridded forecast. This decides whether a line fault counts as a fire, and
  is probably the most dependable thing the sensors do.

  Smoke: a particulate sensor only reacts when the plume passes over it. On a
  windy day a grass-fire plume is a narrow band, so a sensor catches fires
  roughly 3-5 km upwind of it. Placed just north of the riskiest feeders and
  towns (fire-weather winds in Kansas are mostly south and southwest), the
  six sensors watch where fires most often start. Paired with a line fault,
  a smoke reading pages the duty officer without anyone driving out. For
  comparison, a phase 3 camera sees smoke 10-15 miles out (300+ sq mi) but
  costs $1,500-4,000 per node plus a monthly data fee.


5. COMPARISON: DRYAD NETWORKS SILVANET (dryad.net)

  Dryad does not publish prices on its website; it sells on quotes. The
  figures below are the most recent public ones found, from 2023.

  Silvanet part      Price                      What it does
  -----------------  -------------------------  -----------------------------
  Wildfire sensor    EUR 48 list (a Sicilian    Bosch BME688 gas sensor;
                     town reported EUR 100,     detects a smoldering fire
                     likely installed)          within 80-100 m; radio
                                                reaches ~1 km (14 dBm,
                                                built-in antenna)
  Mesh gateway       EUR 371                    Relays for up to 100 sensors;
                                                gateways 2-6 km apart
  Border gateway     EUR 549                    Cellular or satellite link to
                                                the cloud; up to 20 mesh
                                                gateways
  Service            15% a year                 Maintenance and cloud platform

  Dryad's investor materials put the sensor's hardware cost at about EUR 30.

  Why their sensor is cheaper:
  a) It is a sensor, not a relay. Range comes from the separately priced
     gateways. Firewatch's node does both jobs; Dryad's closest equivalent
     is a gateway plus a sensor, about EUR 420.
  b) One gas chip, a few dollars, about 4 mA, measures temperature,
     humidity, pressure, and gases. Our particulate sensor costs $38 and its
     fan draws 60-120 mA.
  c) No battery. The low draw lets a small solar cell and supercapacitors
     run it. A relay that is always listening can't get through a Kansas
     winter night on supercapacitors.
  d) A custom single board in a molded case, built by the hundreds of
     thousands.

  A 450 sq mi district at Dryad's average density (0.2 sensors per hectare):

    116,500 hectares x 0.2  = ~23,300 sensors x EUR 48  = ~EUR 1,120,000
    ~233 mesh gateways x EUR 371                        =    ~EUR 86,000
    ~12 border gateways x EUR 549                       =     ~EUR 6,600
                                                          ---------------
                                                          ~EUR 1,210,000
    plus 15% a year                                     ~EUR 180,000/yr

  Silvanet is sized for forests, where every 100 m matters and air is still
  under the canopy. In open grass with a 25 mph wind, a gas chip would only
  notice a fire very close by.

  Not copied from Dryad: replacing the particulate sensor with the gas chip
  would save about $30 a node and solve the fan's power draw, but it gives up
  the one sensor that notices smoke kilometers away, and Meshtastic reports
  only a raw gas reading from that chip, not Dryad's trained fire detection.


6. WAYS TO LOWER THE COST WITHOUT LOWERING QUALITY

  Lever                                    Effect on the district's cost
  ---------------------------------------  ----------------------------------
  Volunteer Fire Assistance grant          Halves it: ~$1,040 -> ~$520
  Electric co-op pays for nodes near its   Up to the full cost of those nodes
    lines
  Mount at co-op substations               ~$25-35 less per node
  Several counties, or the Kansas Forest   ~5-10% on parts; spares drop from
    Service, buy together                    10% per county to ~3% shared
  Buy tax-exempt                           Avoids ~8-9% sales tax
  Volunteer or Forest Service assembly     No labor cost

  Volunteer Fire Assistance grant. Administered by the Kansas Forest Service
  with US Forest Service funds, for departments protecting areas under 10,000
  people. 50/50 cost share; communications equipment is a stated priority.
  Purchases dated before the application are not reimbursed. The 2027 window
  is May 1 - June 15, 2027: apply first, buy after.

  Electric co-op. Line-caused ignitions are the co-op's liability, and a
  wildfire mitigation plan is the natural place to fund sensors on its
  feeders. The plan already asks the co-op for the line-fault feed.

  Substations. Mains power replaces the solar panel; keep the batteries as
  backup. This improves the node: it stays up through cloudy weeks, and runs
  on battery through the outage that a line fault causes.

  Joint purchase. One order, one shared spare-parts pool, one person flashing
  firmware.

  Sales tax. Fire districts are usually exempt as local government bodies;
  check with the county clerk.

  Bulk buying alone saves little at this scale. These modules already sell
  near volume pricing: one distributor lists the RAK1901 at $8.25 for 1 and
  $7.59 for 1,000, and the RAK4631 at $35.49 for 1 and $32.65 for 1,000.

  Stacked together (grant, joint purchase, shared spares), a county's own
  cost is roughly $450-550. Nodes the co-op pays for come off that.

  Statewide option. All 105 Kansas counties at six nodes each is about 630
  nodes. That could justify one custom board built on RAK's pre-certified
  RAK4630 radio module, which avoids a full FCC certification. A guess:
  $80-100 a node with the same radio, antenna, and range. It takes one-time
  engineering and would be a Kansas Forest Service project, not a county one.

  Keep spending on: the outdoor antenna, the cable, the radiation shield, and
  good battery cells. They set the range and keep the readings accurate, and
  replacing one costs a trip up the tower.


7. OPEN RISKS

- Power. The particulate sensor's fan draws more than a small panel supplies
  running nonstop. Whether Meshtastic firmware sleeps the fan between
  readings is unverified. Check it on one node through a cloudy week before
  buying six.
- Homemade enclosure and shield. Cheap, but a tower visit to fix one costs
  more than the parts saved. Build one and leave it out in weather first.
- Particulate sensor sourcing. Meshtastic reads the I2C version (PMSA003I).
  The cheaper serial version (about $19) is not supported as far as we can
  tell. The bare I2C module is quote-only from distributors.
- Tariffs and shipping. Buying direct from RAK (China) adds a 25% import
  tariff plus shipping; US distributors price that in.
- Range figures are estimates for flat to rolling ground, not measurements.
